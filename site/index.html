              About this site

              An independent project that follows bills once they reach the House or Senate floor: who wrote them, who voted for them and who stopped them. It isn't affiliated with Congress, any party, campaign or advocacy group.

              Everything is pulled from public records and refreshed automatically. The code and every data file are public on GitHub, so anyone can check how a number was produced. Each bill lists the sources behind it at the bottom of its card.

              Spot an error? Report it on GitHub

              How a bill becomes law
              1. IntroducedA member files it in their own chamber. That's why bills are numbered H.R. or S. Tax bills must start in the House.
              2. CommitteeIt goes to the committee for its subject. Most bills never leave this step.
              3. Floor voteThe first chamber passes it by majority. In the Senate, most bills first need 60 votes to end debate.
              4. Other chamberCommittee and a floor vote again, in the House or Senate.
              5. Same textBoth chambers must pass identical wording, by trading amendments or through a conference committee.
              6. PresidentSigns it into law or vetoes it. Unsigned for 10 days (Sundays excluded), it becomes law, unless Congress has adjourned, which kills it.

              A Congress lasts two years, split into two yearly sessions.
              Bills carry over between sessions, but any bill not law by the time a Congress ends dies and has to be reintroduced.

              What gets tracked

              Thousands of bills are introduced each Congress and most never get a vote. This site follows the ones that reach the floor:

              • Always: bills that became law, were sent to or vetoed by the President, passed both chambers, or were voted down or fought over on a roll call (cloture, discharge, a failed passage or motion to proceed).
              • Passed one chamber: only when that passage was a roll call vote, so members are on the record. Voice-vote and unanimous-consent passages are left out.
              • Hand-picked: a few bills are added on purpose at any stage, with notes and sources.
              • Found two ways: by each bill's latest action, and once a day by checking every roll call this Congress, so a vote isn't missed when later paperwork follows it.

              Statuses. Killed means the bill's most recent decisive floor vote failed (cloture, a motion to proceed, passage, a fast-track vote, a discharge attempt or its rule) and nothing has moved it forward since. Routine failed motions, like a motion to recommit, don't count. A killed bill can still be revived until the Congress ends. Vetoed means the President vetoed it and no override succeeded.

              AUTO means a bill's facts come straight from Congress.gov and haven't been reviewed by hand yet. 119 REF marks a bill from an earlier Congress kept for reference.

              How it updates
              • On days Congress meets: floor votes on every update from 8 AM to 11 PM Central and bills about every five hours.
              • During recess: bills about once a day.
              • Senate votes come from Voteview, which usually posts a vote a few hours after it happens. senate.gov turns away automated requests, so this site links to its official vote pages instead of fetching them.
              • House votes and bill histories come from the official Congress.gov API.

              The header shows when the data last changed, so during recess that time can be a day or more old even though checks keep running. Updates run on GitHub's scheduler, which can run hours late or skip runs when GitHub is busy, so a check that's due happens on the next run that does start.

              If Congress.gov stops responding for more than one check, a note under the header says so and goes away on its own once it's back. Bills and votes already on the page stay as they were.

              Glossary
              Roll call vote
              Each member's yes or no is recorded by name. Also called a recorded vote.
              Voice vote
              Members answer aloud together; no names are recorded.
              Unanimous consent
              Passes unless a single member objects. No vote is recorded.
              Cloture
              The Senate's vote to end debate. Most bills need 60 of 100.
              Filibuster
              Using the Senate's unlimited debate to block a final vote.
              Hold
              A senator's notice that they'll object to moving a bill by unanimous consent.
              Suspension
              A House fast track: limited debate, no amendments, two-thirds needed to pass.
              Rule
              A House resolution, written by the Rules Committee, that sets how a major bill is debated. No rule, no debate.
              Discharge petition
              218 House signatures force a vote on a bill leadership won't schedule.
              Reconciliation
              A budget process that needs only 51 Senate votes, limited to taxes, spending and the debt limit.
              Veto override
              Two-thirds of both chambers can pass a bill over the President's veto.
              Pocket veto
              The President doesn't sign a bill while Congress is adjourned, and it dies.
              Lame duck
              The stretch after an election when the outgoing Congress still serves, until Jan 3.
